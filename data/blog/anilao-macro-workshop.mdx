This April 2026, I took a ten-day macro photography workshop with my buddy, Deb Stewart in Anilao Batangas. The workshop was led by Mike Bartick, Dan Bishop and Max Hober at Crystal Blue resort. In this workshop I learned so much but the one thing that I really got better at was using a snoot to photograph macro critters. A snoot helps (what is it.) separate the subject from the background by focusing the light on the subject only.

At first, I had a very diffucult time focusing the light on the tiny subjects but I was determined to make it work. My mantra: Snoot or die!

By the fifth day of the workshop, thanks to the guidance of my divemaster, Kim, I got the technique down.

<Image
  src="/static/images/anilao-macro-workshop-apr2026/sexyshrimp-snoot.jpg"

Using the camera's pipper to focus and compose the shot were the overarching themes of the workshop. I didn't know this word, pipper which refers to the focus box that you use in the viewfinder to achive focus on the subject. I gained confidence with moving the pipper prior to shooting the subject which helps with composition.

I used the pipper along with the snoot to photograph this octopus hiding in its hole. I was able to aim the snoot's light on its head, focus on its eye and with the depth of field, get the texture of its body.

Other topics that were covered in the workshop besides snooting included creating black and blue backgrounds, using bokeh, and back lighting.

The key to achieving a black background is ensuring that there is nothing behind the subject.

<Image
  src="/static/images/anilao-macro-workshop-apr2026/goby-composition.jpg"
  width={800}
  height={840}
  alt="gobey"
/>

Open apertures adds bokeh and separates the subject from the background. In the lionfish shot below, the background is not very interesting. You can see the bokeh below and behind the subject which makes it pop.
